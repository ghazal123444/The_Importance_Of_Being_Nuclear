I am a student in Lebanon, a country that has been living through a severe electricity crisis for years. Power cuts and unreliable electricity are not abstract problems here—they are part of everyday life. I have experienced the effects of this crisis personally, and I am still living through them.

That experience pushed me to ask a simple but important question:

Could nuclear power help solve Lebanon’s electricity crisis?

This project is my attempt to study that question seriously.

The first part of the research evaluates nuclear power as an energy option for Lebanon by comparing it with coal, diesel, solar, and wind in terms of fuel requirements, land use, carbon emissions, cost, and safety.

The second part focuses on one of the biggest engineering concerns for nuclear power in Lebanon: earthquakes. Lebanon is in a seismically active region, so any serious discussion of nuclear energy must also consider seismic safety. This study uses probabilistic seismic hazard analysis and a base-isolated reference nuclear power plant model to examine whether seismic isolation could reduce earthquake demands on nuclear structures along the Lebanese coast.

The goal of this project is not to claim that building a nuclear power plant in Lebanon is simple or immediately feasible. It is to show, through quantitative analysis, that nuclear energy deserves serious technical consideration—and that seismic risk can be studied and reduced through engineering.

This research is part of my effort to understand a problem that has directly affected my life and to explore whether science and engineering can contribute to a long-term solution for Lebanon.
